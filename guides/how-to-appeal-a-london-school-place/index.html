How to appeal a London school place

If your child has been refused a place at the school you wanted, you have a statutory right of appeal. This is what realistically works, what doesn't, and what to expect — based on the published data for all 33 London boroughs from 2016 to 2024.

Last updated 13 May 2026. See our live appeals tracker for borough success rates.

What you need to know first: Reception, Year 1 and Year 2 places (infant classes) are statutorily capped at 30 children per teacher, which makes appeals harder. Secondary appeals are typically easier to win because the legal "infant class size" rule doesn't apply. Across London, secondary appeal success rates run roughly 25–35% depending on year and borough, vs. roughly 15–20% for primary.

What an appeal is, and isn't

A school admission appeal is a formal hearing in front of an independent panel of three people, none of whom work for the school or the local authority. They have the power to overturn a refusal of a place if either (a) the admissions criteria weren't applied correctly to your case, or (b) — in non-infant classes — the harm done by refusing your child a place outweighs the harm to the school of admitting an additional pupil over its planned admission number (PAN).

An appeal is not:

  • A general complaint about the allocation system.
  • A request based on convenience (shorter walk, after-school care, parent's job).
  • A second chance to argue that your preferred school is better than the offered school.

The realistic grounds that win appeals

1. The admissions criteria were misapplied

The strongest argument. Examples that have succeeded:

  • The local authority used the wrong distance measurement (straight-line vs. walking route).
  • A sibling was on roll at the relevant point, but wasn't counted.
  • The faith criteria weren't applied because the SIF was lost or filed late by the school.
  • A child with a medical or social need had documentation that wasn't properly considered.

If you suspect any of this applies, ask the local authority for a written explanation of how your application was assessed before the appeal hearing. They are required to provide it.

2. Significant medical or social need (the "exceptional needs" route)

If your child has a documented medical condition or social need that this specific school is uniquely placed to meet — and that other schools, including the one offered, cannot — this can succeed. You will need clinical or social-services evidence, signed and dated within the last 12 months, that specifically addresses why this school.

3. Prejudice (secondary only)

For Year 3 upwards and all secondary, you can argue that the harm to your child from refusing the place is greater than the harm to the school of admitting one extra pupil. This is the standard route. Panels weigh:

  • How oversubscribed the school is and whether it has accepted appeals in previous years (you can ask for these figures).
  • Whether class sizes, room space, specialist staff, and timetabling can absorb one more child.
  • The specific impact on your child of going to the offered school — travel time, sibling separation, continuity of friendships, mental health.

What does not work

Arguments that panels almost never accept: "We pay our taxes here." "The other school is further away." "My child is bright/quiet/sensitive." "We applied first." "The school refused us last year too." "I work near the school." None of these are appeals grounds, even when true.

Step by step

  1. Read your refusal letter carefully. It tells you which school you can appeal for, the deadline to lodge, and how to do so. Lodging deadline is typically 20 school days from the refusal date.
  2. Decide which schools to appeal for. You can appeal multiple refused schools. Be selective: appeals take time and emotional energy.
  3. Request the school's appeal pack. This usually arrives 7–10 days before the hearing. It tells you the school's PAN, how many children are currently on roll, the published admission criteria, and the school's case for why it cannot take more pupils.
  4. Write your appeal statement. 1–2 sides of A4, no longer. Focus on: facts of your case, evidence, and the specific grounds. Avoid emotion; include facts, names, dates, and document references.
  5. Gather evidence. Medical letters, recent (signed, dated). Distance measurements (the LA's published figure if you have it). Anything that supports a specific argument.
  6. Attend the hearing. Hearings are now usually hybrid (in person or video). You'll have 10–15 minutes to present, then questions. The panel decides within 5 school days. Decisions are binding on the school.

What to put in your statement

Open with one paragraph stating which ground you are appealing under. Three to four paragraphs of evidence. One paragraph on why this school in particular. One closing paragraph on the harm of refusing. That's it. Less is more.

Borough-by-borough context

Appeal success rates vary widely across London boroughs and across years. The data is published annually by the DfE and we maintain a live tracker. As of the latest publication:

  • Primary appeals across London are typically 10–25% successful, with infant-class-size rules suppressing the rate.
  • Secondary appeals typically 20–35%. Some boroughs and individual schools run significantly higher.
  • Variation between schools within a borough is larger than variation between boroughs.

If you lose the appeal

You cannot re-appeal the same school for the same academic year unless there is a material change in your circumstances. You can:

  • Remain on the waiting list — it is reordered each time, so your position changes through the autumn term as families move.
  • Apply for an in-year transfer once the school year has started, if a place opens up.
  • Consider whether the offered school may be better than you initially feared. Visit again, and meet the headteacher.

Frequently asked

Do I need a lawyer or paid advocate?

No. Appeal panels are designed to be accessible to parents without legal training. Lawyers can help but rarely change outcomes — the same arguments work whoever presents them.

Can I appeal for more than one school?

Yes, you can appeal for every school you were refused. Each appeal is heard separately by its own panel.

How long does an appeal take?

From refusal to decision, roughly 6–10 weeks for primary, 8–14 weeks for secondary. Decisions are binding.

If my appeal succeeds, does my offered place go away?

Yes. You accept the appealed school, and the offered place is released to the next family on its waiting list.

What's the difference between an appeal and a waiting list?

You should join the waiting list and appeal — they are independent. The waiting list moves automatically as families decline. The appeal is a one-off formal hearing.
